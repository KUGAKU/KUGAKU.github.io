## Source

- [ACM-CCS-2026/Transparency-Report](https://github.com/ACM-CCS-2026/Transparency-Report)

Introduced in the lab, so I read through it. Less about the substance of peer review and more a document the organizers published explaining **why papers were dropped before the review stage even began**. It makes the pre-submission checklist concrete, so I'm taking notes for myself.

## Breakdown of desk rejects (225 papers)

This is the count of papers that got cut before anyone read the contents.

- **Missing or empty Open Science artifacts** — 122 papers (54.2%)
- **Invalid or hallucinated references** — 41 papers (18.2%)
- **Anonymity violations (double-blind)** — 19 papers (8.4%)

Honestly, I didn't expect more than half to be Open Science related — "the submission package isn't complete" turned out to be a bigger reason than "the contents aren't strong enough."

## The minimum bar at submission time

While reading, I jotted down what I'd want to watch out for when I'm the one submitting.

- **Always attach the Open Science appendix** — the report describes it as a "required, reviewable component." Not optional, treated as a mandatory part of the submission. Code, data, and models need to be available via anonymous access.
- **Verify references** — don't paste in hallucinated references generated by an LLM. Apparently this category surfaced for the first time at CCS 2026, and it's becoming a sign of the times.
- **Be strict about double-blind** — watch for descriptions and self-citations that could identify the authors.
- **Add an "Ethical Considerations" section if there's anything ethically sensitive.**
- **Disclose AI tool usage explicitly.**
- **Declare every conflict of interest** — apparently 49.6% of submissions had missing declarations. Half of submissions is a lot.
- **Don't change registration metadata (title, abstract, track) significantly after the fact.**

## Acceptance rate

- **19.5%** of the 981 papers that reached final review
- **15.8%** of the 1,206 total submissions (including desk rejects)

Even if you make it to final review, it's roughly 1 in 5. Counting the ones cut at the door, it's closer to 1 in 6.

## What stuck with me

I read this while remembering things I'd been told in lectures and through prior experience: "always include references, include the data properly, run comparisons." Cross-checking the report against my own memory, it lined up like this.

- "Include the data properly" overlaps with the Open Science requirement. Beyond just stating claims in the body, **whether you can ship it in a reproducible form** functions as a precondition for acceptance.
- "Always include references" reads differently in the LLM era — it's less about quantity or coverage and more about **not hallucinating, and verifying that things actually exist**. The meaning has shifted.

The thing that stuck with me most after reading through the whole report: **before content quality even enters the picture, nearly 20% are dropped on whether the submission is a complete package**. Doing good research and packaging it cleanly to ship are two different skills, and you have to do both.
