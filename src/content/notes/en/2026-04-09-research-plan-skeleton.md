## Intro

"I think I need some kind of research skeleton" is something I'd been telling myself for a while.

When I read papers, the discussion usually starts from hypothesis testing. But **how did that hypothesis come out?** — that preceding part was always unclear to me. Even when told to "structure a research plan", I couldn't figure out where to begin when building one myself. I kept trying to jump straight to "pick a theme" and kept getting stuck.

A class gave me a chance to organize the full picture, so I'm leaving my own understanding here as a memo.

## Seven steps

Summarizing the class in my own words: a research plan is built in roughly these 7 stages.

1. **A question that wants to solve something new**
   Research starts from a question. A naive curiosity like "I want to know this" or "I don't understand this."
2. **Narrow the question into a sharp one**
   A naive question is too broad to tackle as-is. Trim the target, scope, and method until you have a question that can actually be answered.
3. **Set the narrowed question as the research theme**
   Give the sharpened question a name as a research framework. This is the theme.
4. **From the theme, form a basic hypothesis of "this is probably how it works"**
   Your prediction, expressed in words.
5. **From the basic hypothesis, form a working hypothesis**
   The basic hypothesis is usually abstract, so you break it down into something that can actually be tested.
6. **Do the work**
   Collect data, run experiments — whatever's needed to test the working hypothesis.
7. **Statistics become necessary**
   To tell what the data is actually saying, you need statistical processing.

## Where I got stuck

Listening to all 7 steps, the parts that felt **"this is where it matters"** were `1 → 2` (narrowing) and `4 → 5` (grounding).

### `1 → 2` — Turning a naive question into an answerable one

If you do this sloppily, everything downstream wobbles.

For example, "I want to know about inequality in society" will never end. You have to chip away at it: **which country, which generation, which kind of inequality, measured how?** — one layer at a time. Narrowing the question isn't about making the subject smaller; it's about **getting close enough to a place where you can actually produce an answer**.

### `4 → 5` — Turning an abstract hypothesis into a measurable one

A basic hypothesis is "probably, things will look like this" — a prediction that can be left abstract. But if you don't ground it into **units you can actually measure**, no amount of data collection will tell you what you were looking at.

For a basic hypothesis like "doing X will improve Y", you have to push further: **what are you measuring Y with / how much does it need to move before you call it "improved"?** That is the working hypothesis.

## In short

"Research plan" tends to evoke step 3 — "pick a theme" — as the starting point. But in reality, there's a whole step before it (`1 → 2`) where most of the direction gets decided. I'd been skipping that and trying to pick a theme out of thin air, which is probably why I kept stalling.

Flip side: 6 and 7 are mostly technical work. If 1–5 are in place, your hands can actually move.

## What's next

- Write down my interests as **raw questions (step 1)**
- Practice narrowing them into **sharp questions (step 2)**
- Once they're sharp enough, push them up into **themes (step 3)**

Starting with the back-and-forth between steps 1 and 2.
